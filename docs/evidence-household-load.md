# Evidence: unequal household load and its cost

Why a household chore split is worth supporting, with each figure checked against the original page on
2026-10-02. Each entry says what the source measures and what it does **not** show. None of these
sources test Huddle or any private-matching approach.

## 1. Time: who does the housework (US government data)
**U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, American Time Use Survey, 2025 results (released June 25, 2026).**
On an average day, 87 percent of women and 75 percent of men spent some time doing household
activities (housework, cooking, lawn care, household management). On the days they did, women spent
an average of 2.8 hours and men 2.1 hours.
Source: <https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.htm>
*Shows:* a measured daily time gap in a national diary survey. *Does not show:* stress, or who carries
the planning behind the tasks.

## 2. Fairness felt: satisfaction with the split (Pew Research Center)
Survey of married and cohabiting adults (American Trends Panel; Oct 2020 n=10,332; Jun-Jul 2019
n=9,834), published Jan 25, 2021. In 2019, 49% of men and 39% of women were very satisfied with how
household chores are divided; by October 2020, 55% of men and 38% of women. 59% of women said they do
more of the household chores than their spouse or partner (6% said their partner does more); 46% of men
said these responsibilities are shared about equally, 34% said their partner does more.
Source: <https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2021/01/25/for-american-couples-gender-gaps-in-sharing-household-responsibilities-persist-amid-pandemic/>
*Shows:* a persistent gap in how fair the split feels, and a gap between partners' own accounts.
*Does not show:* the effect on health; the 2020 figures were collected during the pandemic.

## 3. The invisible part: cognitive labor (sociology)
Daminger, A. (2019). "The Cognitive Dimension of Household Labor." *American Sociological Review*
84(4): 609-633. Interviews with 35 college-educated, married couples (70 parents), mostly white or
Asian, about the cognitive work behind household tasks: anticipating a need, identifying options,
deciding, and monitoring results. In 26 of 32 different-sex couples the woman did more of the total
cognitive labor, mostly anticipating and monitoring; deciding was usually shared. Couples often
described their division as collaborative even where this work was uneven.
Summary used for checking: <https://behavioralscientist.org/how-couples-share-cognitive-labor-and-why-it-matters/>
*Shows:* a mechanism (planning and remembering are labor too) from a careful qualitative study.
*Does not show:* how common this is. The sample is small and not representative.

## 4. The cost: association with distress (public health)
Ervin, Taouk, Fleitas Alfonzo, Hewitt & King (2022). "Gender differences in the association between
unpaid labour and mental health in employed adults: a systematic review." *The Lancet Public Health*.
14 studies, more than 66,800 participants: in 11 of the 14, women reported more depressive or
psychological distress symptoms as their unpaid labour demands increased; for men, only 3 of 12
studies found any negative association. The authors call for higher-quality longitudinal research and
more consistent measurement of unpaid labour.
Summary used for checking: <https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/08/220831210017.htm>
*Shows:* a consistent association in observational studies of employed adults.
*Does not show:* causation, or the effect on people who are not in paid work.

## What this supports, and what it doesn't
- **Supported:** the household load is unequal on average by measured time and by perception; a part of
  it is invisible (anticipating and monitoring); and heavier unpaid load is associated with more
  distress in observational research.
- **Not supported by any of this:** that Huddle's approach (private limits, one unattributed plan) reduces
  anyone's load or stress. That remains a hypothesis until real households try it.
- The data above are mostly reported by gender. Huddle is built for anyone who carries most of the load,
  whatever their household looks like.

## Wording that stays inside the evidence
> In the U.S., women spend more time on household activities than men on an average day (2.8 hours
> against 2.1 among those who did any; BLS, 2025), and in Pew's survey of couples 59% of women said they
> do more of the chores while 38% were very satisfied with the split in October 2020. Part of the work
> is invisible: Daminger's interviews with 35 couples found the anticipating and monitoring was done
> more by one partner. Across 14 studies, heavier unpaid labour was linked to more distress among women
> in 11 (Ervin et al., 2022).

Avoid: "chores cause stress", "Huddle reduces stress", or any figure not listed here.
